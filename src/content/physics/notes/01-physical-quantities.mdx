# Physical quantities and measurement

<Key>
A **physical quantity** is something you can measure. Every measurement is a **number and a unit**. Physics uses the **SI** (Système International) units so that everyone's results can be compared.
</Key>

## SI base units you need

| Quantity | Symbol | SI unit | Unit symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| length | $l$ | metre | m |
| mass | $m$ | kilogram | kg |
| time | $t$ | second | s |
| temperature | $T$ | kelvin | K |
| electric current | $I$ | ampere | A |

Other units are built from these. For example speed is m/s, and the newton is $\text{kg m/s}^2$.

## Prefixes

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| giga | G | $10^{9}$ |
| mega | M | $10^{6}$ |
| kilo | k | $10^{3}$ |
| centi | c | $10^{-2}$ |
| milli | m | $10^{-3}$ |
| micro | $\mu$ | $10^{-6}$ |
| nano | n | $10^{-9}$ |

<Mistake>
Converting areas and volumes. $1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm}$ but $1\ \text{m}^2 = 10\,000\ \text{cm}^2$ and $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1\,000\,000\ \text{cm}^3$. You must square or cube the conversion factor.
</Mistake>

## Measuring length

- A **metre rule** measures to the nearest millimetre. Place the scale right against the object and view from directly above to avoid **parallax error**.
- For small lengths use **vernier calipers** or a **micrometer screw gauge**, which read to $0.01\ \text{mm}$ or better.
- To measure something very thin, such as the thickness of a sheet of paper, measure a **stack** of $n$ sheets and divide:

$$\text{thickness of one sheet} = \frac{\text{thickness of stack}}{n}$$

## Measuring volume

- **Regular solid:** use $V = l \times w \times h$.
- **Liquid:** pour into a **measuring cylinder** and read the scale at the bottom of the meniscus with your eye level with it.
- **Irregular solid:** measure the rise in water level when the object is lowered into a measuring cylinder (**displacement**).

## Measuring time

Use a stopwatch or digital timer. Human reaction time (about $0.2\ \text{s}$) is a big source of error for short times, so time a **long interval** or many repeats.

### The pendulum

A pendulum's **period** $T$ is the time for one complete swing (out and back).

1. Time **20 oscillations** with a stopwatch.
2. Divide by 20 to get the period. Timing many swings reduces the percentage error from your reaction time.

$$T = \frac{\text{total time}}{\text{number of oscillations}}$$

<Tip>
"Describe how to find the period accurately" always means: time many oscillations, then divide. Say it, and say why (it reduces the effect of reaction time).
</Tip>

## Scalars and vectors

- A **scalar** has **size only**: distance, speed, mass, time, energy, temperature.
- A **vector** has **size and direction**: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum.

| Scalar | Vector |
|---|---|
| distance | displacement |
| speed | velocity |
| mass | weight |

## Adding vectors (resultant)

The **resultant** is the single vector that has the same effect as two or more vectors acting together.

- **Same direction:** add the sizes.
- **Opposite directions:** subtract the sizes. The resultant points the way of the larger one.
- **At right angles:** use Pythagoras.

$$R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$

<Ext>
For vectors at right angles you may also be asked for the **direction** of the resultant: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$. Alternatively, draw a **scale diagram**: draw the vectors tip-to-tail to a stated scale and measure the length and angle of the resultant.
</Ext>

## Worked example

A boat is pushed east at $3\ \text{m/s}$ by its engine and north at $4\ \text{m/s}$ by a current.

$$R = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\ \text{m/s} \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = 53^\circ \text{ north of east}$$
