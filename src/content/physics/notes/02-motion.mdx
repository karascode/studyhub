# Motion

<Key>
**Speed** tells you how fast something moves. **Velocity** is speed *in a given direction*. **Acceleration** is how quickly the *velocity* changes.
</Key>

## Speed

$$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \qquad v = \frac{s}{t}$$

- Distance in metres (m), time in seconds (s), speed in m/s.
- **Average speed** $= \dfrac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}$. It is *not* the average of the start and end speeds unless acceleration is constant.
- To convert km/h to m/s, divide by $3.6$. For example $72\ \text{km/h} = 20\ \text{m/s}$.

## Acceleration

$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v - u}{t}$$

where $u$ is the initial velocity and $v$ the final velocity. The unit is m/s².

- Speeding up: positive acceleration. Slowing down: **negative** acceleration (deceleration).
- **Uniform acceleration** means velocity changes by the same amount every second.

## Distance–time graphs

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Straight sloping line | constant speed |
| Horizontal line | stationary (at rest) |
| Steeper line | faster |
| Curve getting steeper | accelerating |
| Curve getting flatter | decelerating |

The **gradient** of a distance–time graph is the **speed**.

## Speed–time graphs

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line | constant speed (zero acceleration) |
| Straight sloping line | constant acceleration |
| Curve getting steeper | acceleration increasing |
| Curve getting flatter | acceleration decreasing |

- The **gradient** is the **acceleration**.
- The **area under** the graph is the **distance travelled**.

<Mistake>
A horizontal line on a *distance*–time graph means the object is **stopped**. A horizontal line on a *speed*–time graph means it is moving at **constant speed**. Always read the axis labels first.
</Mistake>

### Areas under a speed–time graph

- A rectangle: distance $= v \times t$.
- A triangle: distance $= \tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$.
- A trapezium: distance $= \tfrac12 (a + b) h$, where $a$ and $b$ are the parallel sides.

## Free fall

Near the Earth's surface all objects fall with the same acceleration if air resistance is ignored: the **acceleration of free fall** $g \approx 10\ \text{m/s}^2$ (it is $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ more precisely).

In air, a falling object has **air resistance** acting against its weight. Air resistance grows as speed grows.

## Terminal velocity

1. At the start only weight acts, so the acceleration is $g$.
2. As speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant force (and acceleration) decreases.
3. Eventually air resistance **equals** weight. The resultant force is zero, so acceleration is zero.
4. The object falls at a constant speed called the **terminal velocity**.

<Ext>
A skydiver reaches terminal velocity, opens a parachute, then **slows down** because air resistance suddenly exceeds weight. Air resistance then falls as speed falls, until a new, much lower, terminal velocity is reached. Describe each stage in terms of forces. On a speed–time graph the curve flattens out at each terminal velocity.
</Ext>

</Tip>

## Worked examples

**1.** A car accelerates from rest to $24\ \text{m/s}$ in $8\ \text{s}$.

$$a = \frac{v-u}{t} = \frac{24-0}{8} = 3\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**2.** A car travels at $20\ \text{m/s}$ for $10\ \text{s}$, then decelerates uniformly to rest in $5\ \text{s}$. Total distance:

$$s = 20 \times 10 + \tfrac12 \times 5 \times 20 = 200 + 50 = 250\ \text{m}$$
