# Turning effects: moments and stability

<Key>
The **moment** of a force is its **turning effect** about a pivot. A bigger force, or a bigger distance from the pivot, gives a bigger moment.
</Key>

## Moment of a force

$$\text{moment} = F \times d$$

- $F$ is the force (N) and $d$ is the **perpendicular distance** from the pivot to the line of action of the force (m).
- Unit: **newton-metre (N m)**.
- Moments are **clockwise** or **anticlockwise**.

Everyday examples: opening a door (push far from the hinges), using a spanner (a longer spanner needs less force), a see-saw, a wheelbarrow, scissors.

<Tip>
The distance must be measured **at right angles** to the force and **from the pivot**. If a force is applied at an angle, use the perpendicular distance.
</Tip>

## Principle of moments

When an object is in **equilibrium** (balanced and not turning):

$$\text{sum of clockwise moments} = \text{sum of anticlockwise moments}$$

It is also true that the **upward forces equal the downward forces**, so the resultant force is zero.

### Worked example: balanced beam

A uniform plank is pivoted at its centre. A $300\ \text{N}$ child sits $1.5\ \text{m}$ from the pivot on the left. How far from the pivot must a $450\ \text{N}$ child sit on the right to balance?

$$300 \times 1.5 = 450 \times d \;\Rightarrow\; d = \frac{450}{450} = 1.0\ \text{m}$$

### Finding an unknown weight (practical)

Balance a metre rule on a pivot. Hang a known weight at one side and an unknown weight at the other and slide them until the rule balances horizontally. Measure the distances from the pivot and use the principle of moments.

## Centre of mass

The **centre of mass** is the point where the whole weight of an object seems to act.

- For a **uniform** regular object it is at the geometric centre.
- A suspended object comes to rest with its centre of mass **vertically below** the point of suspension.

### Finding the centre of mass of an irregular lamina

1. Make three small holes near the edge of the card.
2. Hang it from a pin through one hole, with a **plumb line** hanging from the same pin.
3. Draw a line along the plumb line.
4. Repeat with the other holes. The lines cross at the centre of mass.

## Stability

An object is **stable** if it returns to its original position after a small tilt.

It topples when the vertical line through its centre of mass falls **outside its base**.

Stability is increased by:
- a **wide base**, and
- a **low centre of mass** (put heavy parts at the bottom).

| Stable | Unstable |
|---|---|
| wide base, low centre of mass (racing car, bus with luggage low down) | narrow base, high centre of mass (double-decker bus loaded on the top deck, tall thin vase) |

<Mistake>
Saying a tilted object topples "because it is heavy". The correct reason: the line of action of its weight (through the centre of mass) falls **outside the base**, giving a moment that turns it over.
</Mistake>

<Ext>
At a bus's tipping point the weight acts directly above the edge of the base, so there is no turning moment. Tilt a little more and the weight's moment about that edge turns it over.
</Ext>

## Worked example: moments with several forces

A $2\ \text{m}$ uniform beam of weight $60\ \text{N}$ is pivoted at its centre. A $40\ \text{N}$ load hangs at $0.8\ \text{m}$ left of the pivot. What force $F$ at $0.5\ \text{m}$ right of the pivot balances it?

$$40 \times 0.8 = F \times 0.5 \;\Rightarrow\; F = \frac{32}{0.5} = 64\ \text{N}$$

The beam's own weight acts at the pivot, so it gives no moment.
