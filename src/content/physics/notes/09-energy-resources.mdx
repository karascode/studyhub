# Energy resources

<Key>
A **renewable** resource is replaced as fast as we use it (it will not run out). A **non-renewable** resource is used faster than it forms and will run out.
</Key>

## Where energy comes from

Almost all our energy resources ultimately depend on the **Sun**: fossil fuels are ancient plant and animal remains, wind is caused by the Sun heating the air, and hydroelectric power needs rainwater, which the Sun evaporates. Exceptions: **nuclear** energy (from uranium), **geothermal** (heat from within the Earth) and **tidal** (the Moon's gravity).

## Resources compared

| Resource | Type | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas) | non-renewable | burned to heat water into steam, which turns turbines | reliable, cheap, high energy density | release $\text{CO}_2$ (global warming), acid rain, finite |
| **Nuclear fission** | non-renewable | uranium nuclei split, releasing thermal energy to make steam | no $\text{CO}_2$, huge energy from small mass | radioactive waste, risk of accidents, costly to decommission |
| **Wind** | renewable | wind turns turbine blades connected to a generator | no pollution, no fuel cost | depends on wind, visual and noise impact |
| **Solar cells** | renewable | convert light directly to electricity | no pollution, good in remote places | only works in daylight, large area for little power |
| **Solar heating** | renewable | Sun heats water in panels on roofs | cheap to run | only works in sunshine |
| **Hydroelectric** | renewable | falling water turns turbines | reliable, can respond quickly | dams flood land, harm habitats |
| **Tidal / waves** | renewable | moving water turns turbines | predictable (tides) | expensive, disturbs marine life |
| **Geothermal** | renewable | hot rocks heat water to steam | constant supply | only available in some places |
| **Biofuels** | renewable | burn crops or waste | carbon-neutral overall | uses farmland, still emits $\text{CO}_2$ when burned |

## A power station

1. A fuel (or nuclear reaction) heats water to **steam**.
2. Steam turns a **turbine**.
3. The turbine turns a **generator**, producing electricity.

Energy chain for a coal station: chemical → thermal → kinetic (turbine) → electrical.

## Nuclear fission

In **fission** a large unstable nucleus (e.g. uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei plus more neutrons, releasing energy. The extra neutrons can split more nuclei, so a **chain reaction** can continue. In a reactor the chain reaction is controlled by **control rods** that absorb neutrons.

## Nuclear fusion

**Fusion** is when light nuclei, e.g. hydrogen, join to form a heavier nucleus (helium), releasing energy. It needs extremely high temperatures and pressures.

- **Fusion powers the Sun and other stars.**
- Controlled fusion for power stations on Earth is still being developed.

<Mistake>
Saying a fossil fuel is renewable because "they are made from plants". They take **millions of years** to form, much slower than we use them, so they are non-renewable.
</Mistake>

<Tip>
For "evaluate" questions give one advantage **and** one disadvantage for each resource, and relate them to the situation (e.g. wind needs a windy location).
</Tip>

## Efficiency of electricity generation

The overall efficiency of a power station is limited because much energy is lost as thermal energy in the boiler, condenser and cables. Transmitting electricity at high voltage reduces energy lost in power lines.
