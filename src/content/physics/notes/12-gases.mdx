# Gas pressure, temperature and volume

<Key>
A gas exerts pressure on its container because its particles **collide with the walls**. Each collision gives a tiny force; billions of collisions per second give a steady pressure.
</Key>

## Explaining gas pressure

- Particles move randomly and at high speed.
- When a particle hits a wall it **bounces back**, so its momentum changes. The wall exerts a force on the particle, and by Newton's third law the particle exerts a force on the wall.
- **Pressure** = total force from all the collisions per unit area of the wall.

## Temperature and the Kelvin scale

When a gas is heated the particles' **average kinetic energy increases**, so they move faster.

The **Kelvin (absolute) scale** starts at **absolute zero**, the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the minimum possible energy:

$$T(\text{K}) = \theta(^\circ\text{C}) + 273 \qquad 0\ \text{K} = -273\,^\circ\text{C}$$

A temperature change of $1\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is the same as $1\ \text{K}$.

<Mistake>
In gas questions with temperature, using °C when the relationship needs kelvin. Always convert: $27\,^\circ\text{C} = 300\ \text{K}$. (Kelvin has no degree sign.)
</Mistake>

## Effect of changes on a gas in a rigid container

| Change | What happens to the particles | Effect on pressure |
|---|---|---|
| **Increase temperature** (volume fixed) | move faster, hit walls **harder** and **more often** | pressure **increases** |
| **Decrease volume** (temperature fixed) | same particles in less space, hit the walls **more often** | pressure **increases** |
| **Increase volume** (temperature fixed) | hit the walls **less often** | pressure **decreases** |
| **Add more gas** (same volume) | more particles, more collisions | pressure **increases** |

<Tip>
A full explanation uses **both** ideas: collisions are **more frequent** and/or **harder** (greater change of momentum). Just writing "particles collide more" is not enough for full marks.
</Tip>

## Pressure and volume at constant temperature

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, **pressure is inversely proportional to volume**:

$$p V = \text{constant} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$$

- If you halve the volume, the pressure doubles.
- Pressure and volume can be in any consistent units (both sides use the same).
- On a graph of $p$ against $V$ you get a **curve**; a graph of $p$ against $1/V$ is a straight line through the origin.

### Worked example

A gas at $100\ \text{kPa}$ occupies $6.0\ \text{litres}$. It is compressed slowly to $2.0\ \text{litres}$ at constant temperature.

$$p_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{100 \times 6.0}{2.0} = 300\ \text{kPa}$$

## Evidence and applications

- A bicycle pump becomes warm and the pressure in the tyre rises as the air is compressed.
- Gas cylinders are stored in cool places because heating raises the pressure.
- A sealed can left in a hot car can burst because the pressure rises as the gas heats.

<Ext>
Pressure also increases with temperature at constant volume. In kelvin, $p/T$ is constant for a fixed mass of gas at constant volume. At absolute zero the pressure would be zero, because the particles would have stopped colliding with the walls.
</Ext>
