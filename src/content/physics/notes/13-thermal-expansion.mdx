# Thermal expansion and temperature measurement

<Key>
Most substances **expand** when heated and **contract** when cooled. The particles vibrate or move faster, so they push slightly further apart on average. The particles do not get bigger, but the **material** takes up more space.
</Key>

## Expansion of solids, liquids and gases

| State | Amount of expansion | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Solids** | smallest | strong forces hold particles close, so they only move apart a little |
| **Liquids** | more than solids | forces are weaker |
| **Gases** | largest (at constant pressure) | almost no forces between particles |

## Uses of expansion

- **Liquid-in-glass thermometer**: the liquid expands up a narrow tube.
- **Bimetallic strip**: two metals bonded together. When heated one expands more, so the strip **bends** towards the metal that expands less. Used in **thermostats** and fire alarms: the strip bends and breaks or makes a circuit.

## Problems caused by expansion

Engineers allow for expansion:
- **Gaps in bridges and railway tracks** (or curved joints) so they do not buckle in hot weather.
- **Pipes** have expansion loops.
- **Overhead power cables** sag more in summer.
- Pouring boiling water into a thick glass may crack it: the inside expands before the outside.

<Mistake>
Writing "the atoms expand". The **atoms stay the same size**. It is the **spacing** between atoms that increases.
</Mistake>

## Temperature and thermal energy

**Temperature** is how hot something is (a measure of the average kinetic energy of its particles). **Thermal energy** is the total energy stored in the object. A bath of warm water has **more thermal energy** than a cup of boiling water, even though the cup has a higher temperature.

## Measuring temperature

A thermometer uses a physical property that **changes with temperature**. Two fixed points are used to calibrate (set the scale): **ice point** ($0\,^\circ\text{C}$, melting pure ice) and **steam point** ($100\,^\circ\text{C}$, boiling pure water at standard pressure).

### Liquid-in-glass thermometer (alcohol or mercury)

1. Put the bulb in melting ice and mark the liquid level as $0\,^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Put it in steam above boiling water and mark $100\,^\circ\text{C}$.
3. Divide the length between the two marks into 100 equal steps.

- **Range:** the lowest to highest temperature it can measure.
- **Sensitivity:** how much the liquid moves for each degree. A **narrower tube** or **larger bulb** makes it more sensitive.
- **Linearity:** equal temperature changes give equal changes in length, so the scale is evenly spaced.
- Responds slowly because glass and liquid must warm up.

### Thermocouple

Two different metal wires joined at both ends. If the two junctions are at different temperatures a small **voltage** is produced, which depends on the temperature difference.

- **Advantages:** very wide range, fast response (small junction), can measure very high temperatures and small objects, gives an electrical reading.
- Needs a voltmeter or electronic reader.

<Tip>
In "compare two thermometers" questions use the words **range**, **sensitivity**, **response time** and **linearity**.
</Tip>

## Worked example

A $2.0\ \text{m}$ steel rail expands by $1.2\ \text{mm}$ when the temperature rises. Why are rails laid with small gaps?

- Steel expands when heated.
- Without gaps, the rail would push against its neighbours and **buckle** (bend upwards) in hot weather.
