# Melting, boiling, evaporation and latent heat

<Key>
During a change of state, energy is **supplied or released but the temperature stays constant**. The energy changes how the particles are **arranged and held together**, not how fast they move.
</Key>

## Melting and boiling

- **Melting point:** the temperature at which a solid becomes a liquid. It is the same temperature as the **freezing point** (for a pure substance).
- **Boiling point:** the temperature at which a liquid boils throughout.
- Pure substances change state at **sharp, fixed** temperatures: pure ice melts at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and pure water boils at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$ at normal atmospheric pressure. Impurities make the melting point lower and the boiling point higher.

## Heating curve

If you heat ice steadily, a temperature–time graph looks like this:

1. **Ice warming**: temperature rises.
2. **Flat section at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$**: ice **melting**. Energy goes into breaking the bonds between particles, not into raising their kinetic energy.
3. **Water warming**: temperature rises.
4. **Flat section at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$**: water **boiling**.
5. **Steam warming**: temperature rises.

A **cooling curve** is the reverse: flat sections at the condensing and freezing points, where energy released by the particles forming bonds keeps the temperature steady.

<Tip>
In a "why is the line flat?" question: "**The energy supplied is used to break the forces between particles (overcome the intermolecular forces), not to increase the kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant.**"
</Tip>

## Evaporation

**Evaporation** is the escape of the **fastest (most energetic) particles from the surface** of a liquid, at **any temperature** below the boiling point.

| | Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|---|
| Where | surface only | throughout the liquid (bubbles) |
| Temperature | any temperature | only at the boiling point |
| Speed | slow | fast |

### Cooling by evaporation

The fastest particles leave, so the **average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid falls**, and the liquid **cools**. This is why sweating cools you down and why a wet towel cools a bottle.

### Factors that increase evaporation rate

- **Higher temperature** (more particles have enough energy to escape)
- **Larger surface area**
- **Moving air** (wind carries away the escaped particles, so fewer return)
- **Lower humidity** (drier surrounding air)

## Specific latent heat

<Ext>
The **specific latent heat** $L$ of a substance is the energy needed to change the state of **$1\ \text{kg}$** of it **without any change in temperature**.

$$Q = m L$$

- **Specific latent heat of fusion** $L_f$: solid ↔ liquid. For ice, $L_f = 3.3 \times 10^5\ \text{J/kg}$.
- **Specific latent heat of vaporisation** $L_v$: liquid ↔ gas. For water, $L_v = 2.3 \times 10^6\ \text{J/kg}$.

$L_v$ is much larger than $L_f$ because to boil a liquid the particles must be **completely separated**, which needs far more energy.
</Ext>

### Worked examples

**1.** Energy to melt $0.40\ \text{kg}$ of ice at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$:
$$Q = mL_f = 0.40 \times 3.3\times10^5 = 1.3\times10^5\ \text{J}$$

**2.** A $2.0\ \text{kW}$ heater boils off $0.10\ \text{kg}$ of water already at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$. Time taken (no losses):
$$Q = mL_v = 0.10 \times 2.3\times10^6 = 2.3\times10^5\ \text{J} \qquad t = \frac{Q}{P} = \frac{2.3\times10^5}{2000} = 115\ \text{s}$$

<Mistake>
Using $Q = mc\Delta\theta$ during a change of state. There is **no temperature change**, so $\Delta\theta = 0$; use $Q = mL$ instead.
</Mistake>
