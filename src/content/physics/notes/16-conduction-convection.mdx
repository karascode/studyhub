# Conduction and convection

<Key>
Thermal energy always flows from a **hotter** place to a **colder** place. It travels by **conduction**, **convection** and **radiation**. The first two need particles (a medium); radiation does not.
</Key>

## Conduction

**Conduction** is the transfer of thermal energy through a material **without the material itself moving**. It is the main method in **solids**.

### Particle explanation (non-metals)

1. Heated particles **vibrate more strongly**.
2. They collide with neighbouring particles, passing on kinetic energy.
3. The energy is passed along from particle to particle.

### Metals are the best conductors

Metals also have **free (delocalised) electrons** that move through the structure. When the metal is heated these electrons gain kinetic energy, move quickly, and collide with other particles further along, transferring energy much faster. This is why metals are good conductors.

| Good conductors | Poor conductors (insulators) |
|---|---|
| metals (copper, aluminium, steel) | wood, plastic, glass, wool, air, water, vacuum |

- **Gases** and **liquids** are poor conductors because the particles are far apart and collide less often. A vacuum cannot conduct at all.
- **Trapped air** is a good insulator: the fur of animals, feathers, wool jumpers, double glazing, foam, loft insulation.

### Everyday examples

- Saucepans are metal (conductor) with plastic handles (insulator).
- A metal spoon feels colder than a wooden one at the same temperature because metal conducts energy away from your hand faster.

### Comparing conductors (practical)

Fix rods of different metals to a heated tank, with a drawing pin stuck on each with wax. The wax melts first on the best conductor.

## Convection

**Convection** is the transfer of thermal energy by the **movement of a heated fluid** (liquid or gas). It happens only in **fluids**.

1. A fluid is heated, the particles move apart and the fluid **expands**.
2. The heated part is **less dense**, so it **rises**.
3. Cooler, denser fluid **sinks** to take its place.
4. This sets up a **convection current**.

<Tip>
Always include the **density** idea in a convection explanation: "warmer fluid expands, becomes **less dense**, rises; cooler, **denser** fluid sinks."
</Tip>

### Examples

- **Radiator in a room:** warm air rises from the radiator, cools near the ceiling, then sinks on the far side and returns.
- **Boiling water in a pan:** water at the bottom heats and rises.
- **Sea breezes:** in the day the land is warmer, so air rises over land and cooler sea air flows in.
- A **freezer** is cooled at the top so that cold, dense air sinks through it.
- Put a heater at the **bottom** of a tank to heat the water and a cooler at the **top** to cool it, so that currents are created.

<Mistake>
Saying "heat rises". It is the **hot fluid** that rises because it is less dense. Convection cannot happen in a solid or a vacuum.
</Mistake>

## Reducing energy loss from a house

- **Loft insulation, cavity wall insulation, carpets**: reduce conduction (trapped air).
- **Double glazing**: the air gap and vacuum reduce conduction and convection.
- **Draught excluders**: reduce convection.

## Worked example

Explain why the freezing compartment of a fridge is placed at the top.

- Air next to the freezer is cooled, so it becomes **more dense** and sinks.
- Warmer, less dense air from lower down rises to replace it.
- A convection current cools the whole fridge.
