# Thermal radiation and its applications

<Key>
**Thermal (infrared) radiation** is the transfer of energy by **electromagnetic waves**. It needs **no medium**, so it can travel through a **vacuum**. This is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth.
</Key>

## Emitters and absorbers

All objects **emit** infrared radiation. The hotter the object, the more it emits.

| Surface | Absorbs | Emits | Reflects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dull black** | best | best | worst |
| **Shiny white / silver** | worst | worst | best |

- A **dull black** surface is the best absorber and the best emitter.
- A **shiny, light-coloured** surface is the best reflector and the worst absorber and emitter.

## Factors that affect the rate of emission

- **Surface temperature** (hotter emits more)
- **Surface area** (bigger area emits more)
- **Surface colour and texture** (dull black emits most)

<Tip>
For temperature-difference questions: a large temperature difference between the object and its surroundings increases the **rate** of energy transfer by radiation.
</Tip>

## Comparing emitters: the Leslie cube

Fill a metal cube with hot water. Its four faces have different surfaces (dull black, shiny, white, matt). Measure the **infrared radiation** from each face at the same distance with a detector (a **radiation detector** or thermometer with a blackened bulb). The dull black face gives the biggest reading.

## Comparing absorbers

Place identical objects painted with different surfaces equal distances from a heater and measure the temperature rise in each over the same time. Dull black rises fastest.

## Applications

| Application | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Solar panels** painted black | absorb as much infrared and visible radiation as possible |
| **Fire-fighter / emergency blankets** shiny foil | reflect radiation back to the body |
| **Houses painted white in hot countries** | reflect radiation, stay cooler |
| **Cooling fins on a fridge** are black | emit heat well |
| **Greenhouse** | the glass lets short-wavelength radiation from the Sun in; objects inside re-emit longer-wavelength infrared that the glass reflects and absorbs, **trapping** the energy |
| **Kettles, saucepans** shiny outside | reduce energy lost by radiation |
| **Infrared thermometer and cameras** | detect infrared emitted from hotter objects |

## The vacuum flask

A vacuum flask keeps drinks hot (or cold) by reducing **all three** methods:

- **Vacuum** between double walls: stops **conduction** and **convection** (no particles).
- **Silvered (shiny) walls**: reflect radiation back, reducing radiation **emission and absorption**.
- **Plastic or cork stopper**: poor conductor and stops convection through the top.
- **Glass** is a poor conductor.

<Mistake>
Saying shiny surfaces "reflect heat". They reflect **infrared radiation**. Also, the vacuum stops conduction and convection, but **not radiation**, which is why the walls are silvered.
</Mistake>

## Consequences of thermal energy transfer

Questions often ask you to link the three methods to a situation. Think:
- **How is the energy leaving?** (Through a solid: conduction; through air or water: convection; across space or by emission: radiation.)
- **What reduces it?** (Insulators and trapped air, shiny surfaces, lower surface area, lower temperature difference.)

## Worked example

Why do penguins huddle together in cold Antarctic weather?

- Huddling reduces the **surface area** exposed to the cold air.
- So less energy is lost by **radiation and convection** from each penguin.
